\section{Hosting known quantum systems}\label{sec:hosted}

Part~I states that DMP hosts quantum content. Its information half can carry any quantum content (Sections~\ref{sec:fix}
and~\ref{sec:host}), while its witnesses read only that content exists, as a stabiliser syndrome reads that a code state is present and
not what it encodes (Section~\ref{sec:holo}). We test the operational form of this statement on four configurations with known answers:
an interacting critical spin chain, a free-fermion chain of 4000 sites, a gauge theory with a local constraint, and a quantum algorithm.
All four reproduce the known values within honest uncertainties, the Schwinger-model mass only to 7\% (Table~\ref{tab:hosted:summary}).
This validates the host and our code. It tests no new physics, and no number here is evidence for or against GUID \tagS.

\subsection{Hosting, operationally}\label{sec:hosted:def}

A \emph{configuration} is a pair $(H,\Sigma)$: a Hamiltonian $H$ on a finite Hilbert space and a \emph{syndrome} $\Sigma\ge0$ with
$[H,\Sigma]=0$. A state exists when $\Sigma\ket\psi=0$; the \emph{existence sector} $\mathcal H_E=\ker\Sigma$ is invariant under the
dynamics, and reading $\Sigma$ does not disturb the content. The \emph{host} runs the configuration inside $\mathcal H_E$, by Lanczos
iteration, a Bogoliubov transformation or a gate sequence. Two \emph{witnesses} then decide whether the result exists.
\emph{Witness~T} (the trust half) checks in integer arithmetic that every computational-basis configuration carrying amplitude has
syndrome zero. \emph{Witness~I} (the information half) requires $\bra\psi\Sigma\ket\psi=0$ to rounding accuracy, in floating point, with
unit norm. The two share no code path with each other or with the host (for example a bit-parallel bit count against the host's lookup
table, or a Householder QR against a pivoted LU), and the gate passes only when both pass. Four controls accompany each benchmark:
$[H,\Sigma]=0$ on a small, complete Hilbert space; an injected fault that leaves $\mathcal H_E$, which both witnesses must refuse; two
orthogonal states of $\mathcal H_E$ (two contents), which must receive the same verdict; and, in B4, a fault that changes only the
content, which must pass.

\emph{Uncertainties.} Exact identities are checked to floating-point accuracy. An extrapolated number carries, as $1\sigma$, the largest
change over a family of alternative fits (windows and functional forms) fixed in the code, combined in quadrature with the statistical
error of a weighted fit where there is one. A comparison passes at $\vert\text{deviation}\vert\le2\sigma$.

\subsection{B1: the Heisenberg chain}

$H=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_{i+1}$ acts on spins $1/2$ on a ring, for even $N$ from 8 to 24. The syndrome
$\Sigma=(S^z_{\rm tot})^2$ selects $S^z_{\rm tot}=0$: 2\,704\,156 states at $N=24$, with 33.9 million nonzero matrix elements. The
ground-state energy per site tends to $e_0=\tfrac14-\ln2$ \cite{bethe1931,hulthen1938}. Lanczos gives the two lowest levels. At every $N$
the ground energy agrees to $1.3\times10^{-15}$ per site with a Newton solution of the Bethe equations,
$N\arctan2\lambda_j=\pi I_j+\sum_k\arctan(\lambda_j-\lambda_k)$ with $I_j=-\tfrac{M-1}2,\dots,\tfrac{M-1}2$, $M=N/2$, and
$E_0=N/4-\sum_j2/(1+4\lambda_j^2)$. Conformal invariance gives $E_0(N)/N=e_0-\pi vc/(6N^2)+\dots$ \cite{blote1986}, with $c=1$ and $v=\pi/2$
here, corrected by powers of $1/\ln N$ \cite{affleck1989}. We fit $a+bN^{-2}+dN^{-4}$ on $N\ge12$. The nine alternatives are the windows
$N\ge8,10,14,16$, and all five windows with an added $N^{-6}$ term.

The fit gives $a=-0.4431457(11)$, a deviation of $+1.5\times10^{-6}$ ($1.3\sigma$, $3.3\times10^{-6}$ relative) \chk{hosted:B1}. All nine
alternatives lie above the exact value, as the logarithmic corrections predict, since no power series in $1/N^2$ absorbs them. Read as
$-\pi vc/6$, the fitted $b$ gives $c=1.004$. The triplet gap $\Delta_N=E_1-E_0$ closes: $N\Delta_N$ grows from 4.18 to 4.39 between $N=8$
and 24, the local exponent $-\dd\ln\Delta_N/\dd\ln N$ rises from 0.946 to 0.968, and a fit $\Delta_\infty+\beta_1/N+\beta_2/N^2$ on
$N\ge12$ gives $\Delta_\infty=0.0024$ against $\Delta_{24}=0.183$. The chain is gapless; $N\Delta_N$ approaches $\pi^2/2$ only
logarithmically. \emph{Witnesses:} T counts the up spins of every register index carrying amplitude, and I evaluates
$\langle(S^z_{\rm tot})^2\rangle$ with $S^z_{\rm tot}$ applied site by site. $[H,S^z_{\rm tot}]=0$ exactly on the full $2^{10}$-dimensional
space. The $N=24$ ground state and the orthogonal triplet pass; a single spin flip ($\langle(S^z_{\rm tot})^2\rangle=1$) is refused by
both.

\subsection{B2: the transverse-field Ising ring at $L=4000$}

$H=-\sum_iZ_iZ_{i+1}-g\sum_iX_i$ acts on a ring of $L=4000$ spins, at $g=1$ (critical), $0.5$ and $1.5$. With Majorana operators
$a_j=(\prod_{l<j}X_l)Z_j$ and $b_j=(\prod_{l<j}X_l)Y_j$ one has $X_j=ia_jb_j$ and $Z_jZ_{j+1}=ib_ja_{j+1}$. The bond that closes the ring
carries the parity $P=\prod_iX_i$; for $P=+1$ it becomes an antiperiodic fermion bond \cite{lieb1961,pfeuty1970}. That sector is
$\mathcal H_E$, and only there $H=i\sum_{jk}K_{jk}a_jb_k$, with $K_{jj}=-g$, $K_{j+1,j}=1$, $K_{1L}=-1$ and all other entries zero. At
$L=10$ this reproduces the lowest even-parity eigenvalue of the full spin Hamiltonian to $1.8\times10^{-14}$. The singular-value
decomposition $K=U\,{\rm diag}(s_m)V^{\mathsf T}$ gives $E_0=-\sum_ms_m$ and a Gaussian ground state with
$G_{jk}=\langle ia_jb_k\rangle=-(UV^{\mathsf T})_{jk}$. A block of $l$ consecutive spins has entropy $S(l)=\sum_mh(\tfrac{1+\nu_m}2)$, where
$h(p)=-p\ln p-(1-p)\ln(1-p)$ and $\nu_m$ are the singular values of $G$ restricted to the block \cite{peschel2003,vidal2003}. Each
coupling costs one SVD of a $4000\times4000$ matrix.

With $E(m)$ and $K(k)$ the complete elliptic integrals of the second kind (parameter $m$) and of the first kind (modulus $k$), there are
four comparisons \chk{hosted:B2}.
(a)~$E_0$ equals the finite-$L$ closed form $-\sum_k\sqrt{1+g^2-2g\cos k}$, $k=2\pi(n+\tfrac12)/L$, to $2\times10^{-16}$ relative at all
three couplings. At $g=0.5$ and $1.5$, $E_0/L$ equals $e_\infty=-\tfrac2\pi(1+g)E(4g/(1+g)^2)$ to $4\times10^{-16}$ \cite{pfeuty1970}.
(b)~At $g=1$, $E_0/L-e_\infty=-3.27\times10^{-8}$. The Casimir term $-\pi vc/(6L^2)$ with $v=2$ gives $c=0.5000000052$; the exact
expansion predicts $\tfrac12+7\pi^2/(480L^2)=0.5000000090$.
(c)~At $g=1$ the Calabrese--Cardy form for a ring \cite{calabrese2004}, $S(l)=\tfrac c3\ln[\tfrac L\pi\sin\tfrac{\pi l}L]+c_1'$, fitted on
$100\le l\le2000$, gives $c=0.50000016(26)$ and $c_1'=0.478558$. The uncertainty is the spread over windows starting at 50, 200 or 400 or
ending at 1000.
(d)~At $g=0.5$ and $1.5$ the entropy saturates, $S(L/2)-S(L/8)=2\times10^{-12}$: an area law. The saturated values, 0.6989875284 and
0.3069825111, match the corner-transfer-matrix prediction to $3\times10^{-12}$. That prediction gives a half-infinite chain the
entanglement levels $(2j+1)\epsilon$ for $g>1$ and $2j\epsilon$ for $g<1$, with $\epsilon=\pi K(k')/K(k)$ and $k=\min(g,1/g)$
\cite[Eqs.~4.30--4.34]{calabrese2004}. A block has two cuts, so $S=2S_{\rm half}$ for $g>1$. For $g<1$ the even-parity ground state is a
cat state whose $\ln2$ the two cuts share, so $S=2S_{\rm half}-\ln2$; we derived this before the comparison.

\emph{Witnesses.} The syndrome is the parity, $\langle P\rangle=\det G$, together with purity ($G$ orthogonal). T reads $\det G$ from a
pivoted LU factorisation: parity $+1$, $\vert\ln\vert\det G\vert\vert<10^{-13}$. I reads it from a Householder QR (the reflector count and
the signs of ${\rm diag}\,R$), with $\max\vert GG^{\mathsf T}-\mathbb1\vert\le7\times10^{-15}$. A spin flip $Z_1$ reverses the first row of
$G$; both read $-1$ and refuse. These 4000 qubits are cheap because the state is Gaussian, not because a 4000-qubit state is cheap
(Section~\ref{sec:hosted:shor}).

\subsection{B3: the Schwinger model}

Quantum electrodynamics in 1+1 dimensions with one fermion of mass $m$ and charge $g$ confines. At $m=0$ it is a free boson of mass
$g/\sqrt\pi$, the vector particle \cite{schwinger1962}. We use Kogut--Susskind staggered fermions \cite{kogut1975} on $N$ sites, with open
boundaries and zero background field. Gauss's law, $L_n-L_{n-1}=Q_n$ with $Q_n=\tfrac12(\sigma^z_n+(-1)^n)$, fixes the field on every link,
$L_n=\sum_{k\le n}Q_k$, and leaves \cite[Eq.~2.6]{banuls2013}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hosted:schwinger}
W=\frac{2H}{ag^2}=x\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\bigl(\sigma^+_n\sigma^-_{n+1}+{\rm h.c.}\bigr)+\frac\mu2\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\bigl(1+(-1)^n\sigma^z_n\bigr)
+\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}L_n^2,
\end{equation}
with lattice spacing $a$, $x=(ag)^{-2}$ and $\mu=2m_{\rm lat}/(ag^2)$. The vector mass is $M/g=(W_1-W_0)/(2\sqrt x)$, taken to $N\to\infty$
and then to $x\to\infty$. We run the naive lattice mass $m_{\rm lat}=0$ and the shifted one, $m_{\rm lat}=-g^2a/8$ ($\mu=-\tfrac14$), which
the discrete chiral symmetry of the lattice model identifies as the massless point \cite{dempsey2022}. The shift vanishes as $a\to0$, so
both lattices have the same continuum limit.

\emph{Existence and witnesses.} $\mathcal H_E$ is fixed by Gauss's law at every site with $L_{-1}=0$ (no background) and $L_{N-1}=0$ (no
field beyond the last site), which together imply total charge zero. The host keeps an integer link register $L_0,\dots,L_{N-2}$ for every
basis configuration. T checks $G_n\equiv L_n-L_{n-1}-Q_n=0$ at every site of every configuration carrying amplitude; I evaluates
$\langle\sum_nG_n^2+Q_{\rm tot}^2\rangle$. The ground and vector states at $N=22$ pass. A charge without its field (one spin flipped) and a
flux line without charges (one link raised) give the value 2 and are refused by both. The model with explicit integer links at $N=6$
(1\,075\,648 states; $\vert L_n\vert\le3$ is exact there) checks the elimination itself: $[H,G_n]=0$ for all $n$, and its 20 states with
all $G_n=0$ reproduce the spectrum of \eqref{eq:hosted:schwinger} to $2.5\times10^{-14}$.

\emph{Method.} Lanczos for $N=8,\dots,22$ (705\,432 states at $N=22$) at $x=0.5,1,1.5,2,3,4$. At each $x$ we fit
$M_N/g=w+\alpha N^{-2}+\beta N^{-3}$ on $N\ge12$. The $N^{-2}$ term is the kinetic energy $\pi^2/(2M\ell^2)$ of the vector particle in a box
of length $\ell$, and $N^{-3}$ absorbs a boundary shift of $\ell$. The alternatives are $N\ge14$, $N\ge16$, $\alpha N^{-2}+\beta N^{-4}$,
and $\alpha N^{-2}$ on the last four sizes. Only $x$ with $N_{\max}/\sqrt x\ge10$ (a box at least ten units of $1/g$ long) enter the
continuum fit; all six qualify. That fit is quadratic in $y=1/\sqrt x=ag$, weighted by the inverse squared $N$-extrapolation errors. Its
alternatives are linear on $x\ge1.5$, quadratic on $x\ge1$, cubic, and, for the shifted lattice only, quadratic without the linear term.

\emph{Results} (Table~\ref{tab:hosted:schwinger}). The shifted lattice gives $M/g=0.586\pm0.039$ (statistical 0.016, fit family 0.035),
$0.55\sigma$ from $1/\sqrt\pi$; the naive lattice gives $0.593\pm0.031$, at $0.92\sigma$. The finite-size form passes an out-of-sample
test: at $x=4$ the fit on $N\le22$ predicts the $N=24$ gap as 0.613467, and the computed value (2\,704\,156 states) is 0.613388.

\emph{Precision limits.} Matrix product states give $0.56421(9)$ \cite{banuls2013} and DMRG $0.5642(2)$ \cite{byrnes2002} (as tabulated in
\cite{banuls2013}); our uncertainty is more than 400 times larger. Those methods reach hundreds of sites and need $N\ge20\sqrt x$ for small
finite-volume effects at $x$ from 5 to 600 \cite{banuls2013}. Exact diagonalisation stops at $N=22$, hence at $x\le4$ and $ag\ge0.5$. The
weighted continuum fit is dominated by the coarsest lattice, $x=0.5$: its $N$-extrapolation is the most precise, but a quadratic in $ag$
describes its lattice artefacts worst. This pulls the central value up and sets the 7\% error. With the mass shift, the values at
$1.5\le x\le4$ already lie within 0.5\% of $1/\sqrt\pi$, in line with \cite{dempsey2022}; they are not continuum values, and we claim
nothing from them. We did not attempt the massive model (binding energies known to $5\times10^{-5}$ \cite{banuls2013}) or the chiral
condensate: a continuum error of several per cent cannot test either.

\subsection{B4: Shor's period finding, and the cost of a generic state}\label{sec:hosted:shor}

To factor $N$ we use a counting register of $t=2n$ qubits and a work register of $n=\lceil\log_2N\rceil$ qubits, held as an exact state
vector \cite{shor1997}. The counting register starts in uniform superposition and the work register in $\ket1$. Controlled $U^{2^j}$
follow, with $U\ket y=\ket{ay\bmod N}$ for $y<N$ and $U\ket y=\ket y$ otherwise, and then the inverse quantum Fourier transform, applied
as the exact discrete Fourier transform (it agrees with the explicit gate circuit to $1.8\times10^{-16}$). $U$ permutes $\{y<N\}$, so
$\mathcal H_E$ (unit norm, work register $y<N$) is invariant. An outcome $k$ gives the period $r$ as the first continued-fraction
convergent denominator $q<N$ of $k/2^t$ with $a^q\equiv1$, and a factor $\gcd(a^{r/2}-1,N)$ when $r$ is even and
$a^{r/2}\not\equiv-1\pmod N$, in one run. We host $N=15,21,33,35,39,51,55,57$ with every base $a$ coprime to $N$, and $N=143$ (24 qubits)
with $a=2,3,5,7$: 192 states.

For every $N$ and $a$ the simulated distribution equals Shor's analytic one \cite{shor1997},
$P(k)=Q^{-2}\sum_{x_0<r}\vert\sum_{j<m(x_0)}e^{-2\pi ijrk/Q}\vert^2$ with $Q=2^t$ and $m(x_0)=\lfloor(Q-1-x_0)/r\rfloor+1$, to a
total-variation distance of at most $7.5\times10^{-12}$, and the factors recovered over all bases are exactly the known ones
(Table~\ref{tab:hosted:shor}) \chk{hosted:B4}. The textbook failure appears where it should: for $N=33$ and $57$ with $a=2$ the period is
found but $2^{r/2}\equiv-1$. \emph{Witnesses:} T requires every amplitude with $y\ge N$ to be exactly zero and the norm to be one, and I
evaluates $\langle\Pi_{y\ge N}\rangle=0$ with the projector applied as an operator. All 192 states pass. A faulty multiplier that exchanges
$\ket1$ and $\ket{2^n-1}$ leaves $\mathcal H_E$ and is refused by both in every case. A phase $Z$ on one work qubit changes the content but
not the existence, and both pass it, as a syndrome that never reads content must. A plain $X$ on the top work qubit is not always such a
fault: for $N=15$, $a=2$ it maps the orbit $\{1,2,4,8\}$ to $\{9,10,12,0\}$, inside $\mathcal H_E$.

\emph{The cost of a generic state.} One random layer is $n$ Haar-random single-qubit gates and $n-1$ controlled-$Z$ gates on a dense
vector of $2^n$ amplitudes of 16 bytes. The measured peak memory is the state plus a fixed 16\,MiB buffer: 1.016 times the state at
$n=26$ (1\,GiB). A layer takes 0.0012\,s at $n=10$, 0.67\,s at $n=22$ and 15.6\,s at $n=26$, and the time per gate grows as $2^{1.065n}$
over $n=18$ to 26 (the excess over $2^n$ is cache and memory bandwidth). At $7.3\times10^{-9}$\,s per gate and amplitude on one core,
$n=50$ needs 18\,PB for the state and about 13 years per layer, and $n=4000$ needs $10^{1205.3}$ bytes. The $2n+3=4099$ logical qubits of
Beauregard's circuit for a 2048-bit RSA modulus \cite{beauregard2003} need $10^{1235.1}$ bytes. Structured content stays cheap: B2 hosts
4000 qubits as four dense 128\,MB matrices because its state is Gaussian, and stabiliser circuits run in polynomial time by the
Gottesman--Knill theorem \cite{gottesman1998,aaronson2004}, which Section~\ref{sec:holocode} uses for codes of thousands of qubits.

\subsection{What the benchmarks show}

The host reproduces the known result in all four configurations, and the witnesses accept exactly the existence sector, refuse every
injected fault that leaves it, and give the same verdict for different contents: the syndrome reading of Section~\ref{sec:holo}, on
systems far larger than a five-qubit code \tagS. Three limits belong to the record. First, these are classical emulations of hosting, and
a classical host carries generic content only at $2^n$ cost; the Existence Turing machine of Section~\ref{sec:host} needs a quantum
information half once its content is generic and large. Second, the precision of B3 is set by exact diagonalisation, not by the host.
Third, nothing here tests a prediction of GUID.

\paragraph{Choices made after seeing the data.} (i)~In B1 the fit family and the $2\sigma$ pass rule were fixed after a prototype had
shown the extrapolated value; under a $1\sigma$ rule B1 would fail ($1.3\sigma$). (ii)~In B2 the window family of the entropy fit was
fixed after a prototype had shown $c=0.500000$ to six digits. (iii)~In B3 the $x$ grid and $N_{\max}=22$ were set from the cost of
exploratory runs, before any continuum fit. The fit rules were written before the first continuum fit (on $N\le18$) and kept, with one
exception: the alternative without a linear term was then dropped for the naive lattice only, whose mass shift is an $O(a)$ effect
\cite{dempsey2022}. Keeping it widens the naive uncertainty from 0.031 to 0.075 (recomputed from Table~\ref{tab:hosted:schwinger}), and the
comparison passes either way. (iv)~In B4 the fault was changed from an $X$ on the top work qubit to the faulty multiplier once the former
turned out not always to leave $\mathcal H_E$.

\paragraph{Caveats.} The modular multiplications of B4 are exact permutation gates, not elementary-gate circuits, which changes gate
counts but not states; the registers use the textbook $t=2n$. B1 stops at $N=24$, the memory limit of a sector code without translation
symmetry. Wall times are single-core on a shared machine and are reported, not checked; all other numbers are deterministic. All numbers
in this section come from \url{docs/simulation/hosted_benchmarks.py} (53 of 53 checks, 492\,s on one core, peak memory 1.9\,GB).

\paragraph{Verdict.} The hosting construction \vd{holds} on four hard configurations with known answers: the Heisenberg energy to
$1.5\times10^{-6}$, the Ising central charge at 4000 sites to $2\times10^{-7}$, the Schwinger mass to 7\%, and Shor's distributions to
$10^{-11}$, while the two witnesses accept exactly the existence sector and never read content \tagS. This validates the host and our
code; it tests no new physics, and every prediction of GUID remains \vd{untested} by it. On a classical host generic content costs $2^n$,
so the Existence Turing machine is cheap only for structured content.
